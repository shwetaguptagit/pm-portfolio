Convert · Product lead, end to end · INMA Award, Best Product Iteration

Checkout 2.0: clarity beat persuasion

A rebuild of the offer page and payment flow across every brand, based on what users needed to know before buying.

+28%
checkout conversion
+16%
post-purchase revenue

The problem

46% of users never scrolled far enough to see the call to action. The pages repeated the same benefits, had more than one button competing for attention, and left real questions unanswered. Every brand had a different journey.

What research showed

Interviews and behaviour data pointed to the same cause: people were confused, not unconvinced. One New Yorker reader didn't know why we asked for a mailing address. It was for a free tote bag, but nothing said so.

What I did

  • Cut the offer page down to what makes each plan different, with one call to action always on screen.
  • Added an FAQ built from real support questions.
  • Simplified payment: slim order summary, email asked earlier, autofill and address lookup, and a review step before charging.
  • Rebuilt it all as reusable components, so every brand gets the fix.

What I owned

Finding the opportunity in the data, running the user research, designing the A/B tests, getting every stakeholder aligned, and the launch plan, training and docs.

What I'd do differently: we changed the whole journey at once. It shipped as one clean experience, but it was slower and harder to tell which change did what. Next time: smaller releases, one change at a time.

Convert · Personalisation · Product lead, end to end

Dynamic monetisation: the right ask for each visitor

A machine-learning score that decides, per visitor, whether to ask for a subscription, a free sign-up, or nothing at all.

+8%
direct revenue
3
revenue lines measured together

The problem

AI search summaries were cutting page views, and every visitor saw the same paywall. Ask a one-time reader to subscribe and they leave, so we earn nothing, when an ad would have earned something.

How it works

Behaviour and content signals score each consented, non-subscribed visitor. The score picks the experience: low intent sees ads only, medium intent gets a free registration gate, high intent sees the paywall. Scores are computed ahead of time, with a safe fallback if scoring is down.

What I owned

  • Which signals the score uses: content category, visit frequency, page depth and traffic source.
  • Success metrics across subscriptions, ads and sign-ups, and the dashboard to watch them.
  • The fallback path if the model behaved unexpectedly.
  • Agreement across subscription, advertising and engagement leads, so no team gained at another's cost.
Scope call: cross-device matching existed in-house but I kept it out of the first release. That removed a dependency on another team and got us to market faster.

The lift was proven against a parallel campaign with identical targeting but no score-based decisions, so the +8% is attributable.

Convert · Personalisation · Product lead

Bringing back people who left checkout

A native, on-site win-back experience for users who started checkout and dropped off, replacing a third-party vendor that was being switched off.

24% → 39%
conversion contribution vs the vendor

The problem

A vendor tool drove about 24% of its conversions through an abandoned-cart banner. The vendor was being removed. Without a native version, we would lose that revenue and the ability to test or personalise it.

What I did

  • Built a native banner, nav button ("Complete order") and in-article unit for users in the abandoned-cart segment.
  • Split users by behaviour: people who only looked get a simple reminder; people who changed or progressed an offer are sent straight back to that exact offer.
  • Kept discounts for high-intent users only, with a countdown. Most people convert without one, so a blanket discount would waste margin.
  • Tagged every link with a source so conversions were measured correctly.
Data behind the split: about 90% of abandoned users (91% for The New Yorker, 89% for WIRED) only viewed an offer. The high-intent group was small, so targeted discounts carried little risk.

Platform · Product lead, 0 to 1

One self-serve growth platform for 9+ brands

A configurable platform for paywalls, offers and checkout that non-technical teams run themselves, across every brand and market.

2 wks → mins
to launch a campaign
~2 mo → 1 wk
to launch a new market

The problem

We weren't serving one customer. Each of 10+ brands and 12+ markets had its own rules, look and marketing plan. On the old vendor platform, every campaign change needed engineering.

What I did

Split every requirement into layers. Define a rule once, and every layer below inherits it. Partner teams could then change placement, gating, pricing and design on their own.

Connected end to end

Chargebee subscriptions, Stripe and PayPal payments, Sailthru email and in-house entitlements, so every purchase ran automatically from offer to access.

Result

Served millions of site visitors a day, supporting 2.2M active subscribers and about $100M in revenue.

Acquire · Product lead, 0 to 1

Partner platform: build once, onboard many

Selling subscriptions inside a partner's own checkout, like Klarna or Verizon, instead of handing out coupon codes.

$5M+
partner pipeline unblocked
~12 wks → days
target time to add a partner

The problem

Partners were ready to sell bundles, but onboarding ran on manual coupons and redemption links. Buyers had to redeem a link and enter card details a second time. Each new partner took about 12 weeks of custom work.

What I owned

  • Research on how others sell bundles through partner sites.
  • The full buyer journey, and where to draw the first-release line.
  • The event design: what data we need, and which subscription states we act on.
  • Requirements for security and duplicate-proof events. Engineering chose the exact mechanism.
  • Partner and engineering conversations, test plan and launch timeline.
Trade-off I made: removing manual redemption also removed a consent step on our side, which limits post-purchase marketing. I accepted that to get to revenue faster.

    How I work

    Find the gap, prove the fix, then scale it

    The same loop runs through all of my work, from a paywall test to a new platform.

    Find the gap

    Funnel data, session behaviour, user interviews, support tickets and reviews.

    46% never saw the button, so the fix was clarity, not more copy.

    Decide what matters

    Size the opportunity, agree success metrics, and cut scope to what proves the idea.

    Kept cross-device matching out of v1 to remove a team dependency.

    Prove it

    A/B tests with a clean control, and guardrails so one metric doesn't win at another's cost.

    Measured subscriptions, ads and sign-ups together.

    Ship and scale

    Reusable components and self-serve tools, so one win reaches every brand and market.

    Campaign launches went from 2 weeks to minutes.

    What didn't work, and what I learned

    Reversed in production

    Pay button first on the paywall

    We put Apple Pay and Google Pay as the first thing users saw. Drop-off went up: people needed to know what they were buying. We put the value first and payment second, and mobile conversion rose 21%.

    Stopped

    Consent or pay

    We let readers share data instead of paying. Users disliked it, drop-offs rose, and it didn't make up the lost revenue. The idea was built well; the assumption behind it was wrong, and data showed that fast.

    Trade-off

    Gift subscriptions before the holidays

    International print gifting would have put the launch date at risk. Data showed it was a small group. I launched without it, sent those users to a working legacy path, and the launch made $600K in about 8 weeks.
